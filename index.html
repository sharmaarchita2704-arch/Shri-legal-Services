Who We Are

A practice built around
what people don't ask.

Before You Marry is an India-based legal advisory practice dedicated exclusively to matrimonial and family law. We are a team of practising advocates with a legal network spanning multiple jurisdictions across the country.

Our work covers the full spectrum of marriage-related legal matters: from prenuptial planning and financial structuring before marriage, to advisory and representation during difficult marriages, to post-divorce settlements and enforcement. Every advocate on our team specialises in this area of law, which means clients receive advice that is specific, informed, and grounded in the realities of Indian family law rather than generalised guidance.

We are not a general practice. We exist specifically for individuals approaching marriage, navigating a difficult one, or working through separation. We advise on what the law actually provides, not what people assume it provides. The gap between those two is often where disputes arise.

Our approach is straightforward: we provide clear, honest legal counsel on matters that carry significant personal and financial consequences. Clients who consult us early are consistently better positioned than those who seek legal advice after a situation has already deteriorated.

Areas of Practice

Six areas.
One focused practice.

We advise and represent clients across every dimension of matrimonial law, from the period before marriage to the conclusion of post-divorce proceedings.

Mutual and Contested Divorce

Comprehensive legal representation in both mutual consent and contested divorce proceedings. We advise on procedural timelines, documentation, settlement drafting, and court representation at all relevant stages.

Prenuptial Agreements

Drafting and legal review of prenuptial agreements under Indian law. We advise on enforceability, what may and may not be contractually stipulated, and how to structure agreements that carry appropriate legal weight.

Alimony and Maintenance

Advisory and litigation in matters of interim maintenance, permanent alimony, and maintenance revision petitions. We represent both claimants and respondents and advise on settlement structures appropriate to each case.

Property and Asset Division

Legal counsel on matrimonial property disputes involving jointly held assets, home loans, ancestral property claims, and streedhan recovery. We advise on the legal position of assets before and after marriage.

Domestic Violence and Protection Orders

Filing and representation under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005. We assist in obtaining Protection Orders, Residence Orders, and Monetary Relief through a confidential and structured process.

Child Custody

Representation in custody, visitation, and guardianship matters, whether contested or by consent. We advise with the child's welfare as the primary consideration, while protecting the parental rights of our clients within Indian family law.

No. Alimony is not automatic in a mutual divorce. It must be explicitly agreed upon by both spouses as part of the settlement terms. If the petition states "no alimony" without both parties having actively discussed and agreed to this position, it becomes a point of dispute during or after the drafting stage. Mutual consent requires genuine agreement on all terms, including whether alimony will or will not be paid, the amount if payable, and whether any other financial claims remain outstanding between the parties.
Yes. Employment does not automatically disqualify a spouse from claiming maintenance or alimony under Indian law. Courts consider the lifestyle maintained during the marriage, the income disparity between spouses, the standard of living enjoyed, and the duration of the marriage. A spouse who earns less than the other may still be entitled to alimony that partially bridges that gap, particularly in marriages of long duration or where primary custody of children is involved.
In a mutual consent divorce, both spouses agree to separate and jointly file a petition. The process involves two court hearings separated by a mandatory cooling-off period and typically concludes within six to eighteen months. A contested divorce is filed by one spouse when the other does not consent, requires establishing specific legal grounds such as cruelty, desertion, or adultery, and may take several years to conclude. Mutual divorce is considerably faster and less adversarial, but is only viable when all financial and custody terms have been genuinely settled between the parties prior to filing.
Yes. The Supreme Court has held that the six-month cooling-off period under the Hindu Marriage Act is not mandatory and may be waived by the court in appropriate circumstances. These include situations where the parties have already been living separately for a substantial period, all terms of settlement are fully agreed, and the marriage has irretrievably broken down. This is not automatic relief. A formal application must be placed before the court with sufficient grounds, and the court exercises this discretion on the facts of each case.
Transfers made to a groom's personal bank account for wedding-related expenses have, in a number of matrimonial disputes, been presented as evidence of dowry payments. This is a recognised pattern in matrimonial litigation. We generally advise families to make direct payments to vendors wherever possible, rather than routing funds through personal accounts. Direct payments create financial transparency, establish proper receipts and documentary records, and significantly reduce exposure to dowry-related allegations or financial disputes in later proceedings.
In certain circumstances, rented accommodation may reduce the risk of residence-right disputes and property-related conflicts in the event of matrimonial or Domestic Violence proceedings. Under the Domestic Violence Act, residence-related claims may arise even where the property belongs to in-laws or other relatives of the husband. Rented accommodation can limit ownership-related disputes, help protect family-owned property from litigation, and simplify practical arrangements during separation. This is not a universal recommendation and depends on the specific circumstances of each couple.
Prenuptial agreements occupy a grey area in Indian law. There is no dedicated legislation governing them, and courts retain discretion in matrimonial matters. Some courts have upheld prenups as valid contracts under the Indian Contract Act where they are not contrary to public policy and were entered into freely by both parties. However, enforceability is not guaranteed. Prenuptial agreements are most effective when they are specific, balanced, and properly documented, and are best understood as a structured legal framework for financial clarity rather than an absolute contractual guarantee.
Yes. The Supreme Court has recognised the right of an accused person to receive a copy of the FIR, particularly in matters affecting personal liberty. Where the police decline to provide the FIR copy, an application may be filed before the jurisdictional Magistrate, relying upon the Youth Bar Association judgment of the Supreme Court. As per the legal position established by that judgment, the FIR copy should ordinarily be supplied within 48 hours of the Magistrate's direction. FIRs involving sexual offences are not uploaded publicly, in order to protect the identity and privacy of the complainant.
Filing aggressive cases or sending hostile legal notices without first attempting mediation or structured discussion frequently escalates matrimonial disputes unnecessarily. Wherever possible, we encourage pre-litigation mediation to explore the possibility of a negotiated settlement. Mediation reduces adversarial allegations between parties, encourages structured resolution, and may facilitate a mutual divorce where both parties are willing to engage constructively. A well-negotiated outcome is in most cases preferable to contested litigation, both in terms of time and final result.
Indian courts determine child custody on the basis of the best interest of the child. Factors considered include the child's age, emotional bond with each parent, stability of the home environment, each parent's ability to provide appropriate care, and where the child is of sufficient age, the child's own preference. Sole custody, joint custody, and shared parenting arrangements are all possible outcomes depending on the circumstances of the case. Custody is not determined by the gender or income of a parent alone.
Yes. Section 24 of the Hindu Marriage Act provides for interim maintenance and is gender-neutral in its application. Either spouse may apply for maintenance from the other during the pendency of matrimonial proceedings, where they are unable to maintain themselves and have no independent income. While this provision is less commonly exercised by husbands, it constitutes a valid legal entitlement under current Indian law and courts have granted it in appropriate cases.
India does not operate a matrimonial property regime that automatically divides assets equally upon divorce. Courts consider whose name the property is registered in, who contributed financially to its acquisition, and the nature of the asset. Under Section 27 of the Hindu Marriage Act, courts have discretion to settle property disputes in the course of divorce proceedings, and outcomes vary considerably depending on the specific facts. Legal advice on property matters should be obtained before separation terms are finalised.
A joint home loan is not resolved automatically by divorce proceedings. The lender is not a party to the divorce, and both co-borrowers remain legally liable for repayment regardless of their marital status. Available options include one party acquiring the other's share and refinancing the loan solely in their name, selling the property and distributing the proceeds, or incorporating a structured settlement into the divorce decree. Leaving this matter unresolved after separation is among the more common and consequential financial oversights encountered in matrimonial practice.
Streedhan refers to all gifts and property received by a woman, before, during, or after marriage, from her family, husband, or in-laws. This includes jewellery, cash, household articles, and property gifted specifically to her. Streedhan belongs exclusively to the wife and cannot be claimed by the husband or his family at any point. A wife is legally entitled to recover her streedhan at any time, and courts regularly direct its return as part of divorce settlements or in standalone civil recovery proceedings.
Under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, domestic violence includes physical abuse, sexual abuse, verbal and emotional abuse, and economic abuse. Economic abuse, which includes withholding funds, controlling finances, or denying access to resources, is specifically recognised under the Act. The law extends to women in marriages as well as those in qualifying live-in relationships. Available relief includes Protection Orders, Residence Orders, Monetary Relief, and Custody Orders, all of which may be sought through a single application before a Magistrate's Court.
A Protection Order under the PWDVA is applied for before a Magistrate's Court. In situations of immediate danger, an ex-parte interim Protection Order may be obtained on the same day or within 24 to 48 hours, without the requirement for the other side to be heard. A police FIR is not a prerequisite, though one may be filed concurrently if appropriate. The application process is structured and can be initiated promptly with the assistance of a practising advocate.
Under the Hindu Marriage Act, recognised grounds for contested divorce include cruelty (physical or mental), adultery, desertion for a continuous period of not less than two years, conversion to another religion, unsoundness of mind, leprosy, venereal disease, renunciation of the world, and presumption of death. Cruelty is the most frequently invoked ground and has been interpreted broadly by Indian courts to include sustained mental harassment, financial deprivation, and conduct that renders cohabitation unreasonable.
If a spouse fails to appear after being properly served with court summons, the court may proceed ex-parte and pass orders based on the evidence presented by the attending party. Repeated absence can result in the matter being decided against the party who does not appear. Service of summons must be properly established before ex-parte proceedings can commence. Proper legal representation at all hearings remains important, as material orders or interim directions may be passed at any stage, including at early or unexpected hearings.
Under the Hindu Marriage Act, there is a 90-day period following the divorce decree during which either party may file an appeal. Remarriage before this period expires, or before any pending appeal is disposed of, may give rise to legal complications. Where neither party has filed an appeal and the decree has attained finality, remarriage may proceed. We advise clients to confirm the precise legal status of their decree before taking this step.
The majority of legal complications that arise during or after a marriage originate in decisions made at the outset, not at the end. How assets are titled, what financial obligations are entered into jointly, and what is understood or misunderstood about the legal consequences of marriage are all matters determined before or at the time of marriage. A structured pre-marriage legal consultation enables informed decision-making at a stage when the range of available options is widest and the consequences of misunderstanding are most readily avoided.
